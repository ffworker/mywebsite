What I Bring

Useful infrastructure behavior beyond tool familiarity

Practical infrastructure ownership

I work across Linux hosts, VPS systems, Raspberry Pi infrastructure, Docker, k3s, DNS, private networking, tunnels, storage, monitoring, and Git-backed configuration. I care whether the service actually works after the diagram looks good.

Structured troubleshooting

I trace problems through the layer that is actually failing: DNS, routing, ports, containers, Kubernetes objects, storage, logs, events, or application behavior. I prefer evidence over guesses and rollback plans over heroics.

I use dashboards, probes, node metrics, kube-state metrics, and health checks to turn "it feels broken" into something inspectable. Monitoring is most useful when it helps decide what to check next.

Repeatability and documentation

I turn one-off fixes into runbooks, command references, Ansible starts, and desired-state repos. Good documentation is not decoration; it is how a system becomes safer to operate next week.

AI-assisted Work

Fast help, fallible output, human responsibility

I work with AI agents like capable colleagues who can inspect, draft, compare, and document quickly. That is useful, but it is not authority. The authority is the live system: logs, metrics, diffs, commands, health checks, and whether the service still works after the change.

What the AI is good at

  • Breaking vague infrastructure goals into inspectable stages.
  • Drafting manifests, scripts, dashboards, runbooks, and migration plans.
  • Comparing approaches and reading more context than one person can hold at once.
  • Producing useful documentation while the work is still fresh.

Where I do not trust it blindly

  • It can miss local context, misunderstand an imprecise problem, or solve the wrong thing neatly.
  • It can produce plausible commands that are unsafe in the current environment.
  • It can overcomplicate a small change or forget an earlier constraint.
  • It still needs someone who understands the outage risk before applying the patch.

My part of the workflow

  • Provide context, constraints, and the actual objective.
  • Challenge assumptions before touching live services.
  • Test against real commands, logs, metrics, rollouts, and URLs.
  • Keep configuration and decisions in Git so the work can be reviewed.

Why this matters professionally

The useful skill is not copying commands. It is coordinating fast technical help while keeping enough judgment to notice when the answer is wrong, too risky, or missing the operational point.
